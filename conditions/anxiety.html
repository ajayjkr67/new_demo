What are anxiety disorders?

Anxiety disorders are the most common mental health conditions in the world. Unlike normal stress or worry, anxiety disorders involve fear and apprehension that is persistent, excessive, and disproportionate to the actual situation — and that significantly impairs daily functioning.

The word "anxiety" covers several distinct conditions, each with its own pattern of symptoms and treatment approach.

Types of anxiety disorders

  • Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD) — chronic, uncontrollable worry about multiple areas of life (work, health, family, finances) that persists even when there is no specific threat
  • Panic Disorder — recurrent unexpected panic attacks (intense surges of fear with physical symptoms like racing heart, chest pain, breathlessness) and persistent worry about future attacks
  • Social Anxiety Disorder — intense fear of social situations due to worry about being judged, embarrassed, or humiliated; can severely limit work and relationships
  • Specific Phobias — marked fear of specific objects or situations (flying, heights, needles, animals) that is disproportionate and leads to avoidance
  • Agoraphobia — fear and avoidance of situations where escape might be difficult, often developing after panic attacks
  • Separation Anxiety — excessive fear of being separated from attachment figures; occurs in both children and adults

Common symptoms

Across anxiety disorders, common symptoms include:

  • Excessive, hard-to-control worry
  • Restlessness or feeling on edge
  • Fatigue and difficulty concentrating
  • Muscle tension
  • Sleep disturbance
  • Physical symptoms: racing heart, sweating, trembling, shortness of breath, dizziness, nausea
  • Avoidance of feared situations

Treatment

Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) is the most evidence-based psychological treatment for anxiety disorders. It works by identifying and changing the thinking patterns and behaviours that maintain anxiety, and gradually reducing avoidance through exposure.

Medication — SSRIs and SNRIs are first-line medications for most anxiety disorders. They are not addictive and work best when combined with therapy. Short-term benzodiazepines may be used in acute situations but are not long-term solutions.

Most patients see meaningful improvement within 8–12 weeks of starting treatment.

An important note on avoidance

Avoidance provides short-term relief but makes anxiety worse over time. Every time you avoid something due to anxiety, the brain reinforces the message that it is dangerous. Effective treatment involves gradually facing feared situations — with support and a proper structure.
