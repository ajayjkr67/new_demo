What is psychosis?

Psychosis is a symptom — not a diagnosis in itself — characterised by a loss of contact with reality. It can occur in schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, severe depression, substance use, and certain medical conditions. The two core features are hallucinations and delusions.

Hallucinations are perceptions without a stimulus — most commonly hearing voices (auditory hallucinations), but can also be visual, tactile, or olfactory.

Delusions are fixed false beliefs held with certainty despite evidence to the contrary — for example, believing one is being followed, that messages are being sent through the television, or that one has special powers.

What is schizophrenia?

Schizophrenia is a chronic psychotic disorder characterised by positive symptoms (hallucinations, delusions, disorganised thinking and speech), negative symptoms (flat affect, reduced speech, loss of motivation, social withdrawal), and cognitive symptoms (problems with memory, attention, and executive function).

Schizophrenia typically emerges in late adolescence or early adulthood. It is not caused by poor parenting, moral failure, or "madness" in the popular sense. It has a significant genetic component and involves dysregulation of dopamine and other neurotransmitter systems.

Early warning signs

Early intervention in psychosis leads to significantly better outcomes. Warning signs include:

  • Social withdrawal and isolation
  • Decline in academic or work performance
  • Unusual or magical thinking
  • Increased suspiciousness or paranoia
  • Neglect of personal hygiene
  • Hearing or seeing things others don't
  • Disorganised or incoherent speech

Treatment

Antipsychotic medications are the cornerstone of treatment. Modern atypical antipsychotics are significantly better tolerated than older medications and effectively reduce positive symptoms in most patients.

Psychosocial interventions — psychoeducation for patient and family, cognitive remediation, supported employment — improve long-term functioning significantly.

Family support and education is critical. The family environment is a major factor in relapse prevention. Understanding the condition reduces expressed emotion and improves outcomes.

For families

If a family member is showing signs of psychosis — unusual beliefs, hearing voices, social withdrawal, dramatic behaviour changes — please seek psychiatric evaluation urgently. Early treatment is the single most important factor in long-term outcome. Psychosis is a medical emergency, not a phase.
