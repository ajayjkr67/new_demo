Types of sleep problems

  • Insomnia — difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking too early, with daytime consequences. The most common sleep disorder.
  • Hypersomnia — excessive sleepiness despite adequate nocturnal sleep; can be a symptom of depression or other conditions.
  • Circadian rhythm disorders — mismatch between internal sleep-wake cycle and the environment (e.g., delayed sleep phase syndrome — consistently sleeping very late and waking very late).
  • Sleep problems secondary to psychiatric conditions — depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, PTSD, and ADHD all commonly cause significant sleep disturbance.

The relationship between sleep and mental health

Sleep and mental health have a bidirectional relationship. Poor sleep worsens depression, anxiety, and cognitive functioning. Depression, anxiety, and other conditions disrupt sleep. This cycle can be self-perpetuating and is one of the reasons mental health conditions are so difficult to recover from without treatment.

In many patients, addressing sleep is a key part of overall psychiatric treatment — not a secondary consideration.

Assessment

A proper assessment involves understanding the pattern of sleep difficulties, sleep history, daytime functioning, medication use, and ruling out underlying psychiatric or medical conditions. A sleep diary kept for 1–2 weeks prior to consultation is extremely helpful.

Treatment

CBT for Insomnia (CBT-I) is the recommended first-line treatment for chronic insomnia. It is more effective than sleeping medication for long-term outcomes and involves sleep restriction, stimulus control, cognitive restructuring, and sleep hygiene education.

Medication — short-term use of sleep medications may be appropriate in certain situations. However, long-term reliance on benzodiazepines or Z-drugs is not recommended due to tolerance, dependence, and cognitive side effects.

Treating the underlying condition — when sleep problems are secondary to depression, anxiety, or another condition, treating the primary condition often resolves the sleep problem.

When to seek help

If you have had sleep difficulties for more than a month that are affecting your daytime functioning — concentration, mood, energy, work performance — please seek evaluation. Chronic insomnia does not resolve on its own for most people, and the longer it continues, the more entrenched it becomes.
