What is OCD?

OCD consists of two components that occur together: obsessions and compulsions.

Obsessions are unwanted, intrusive thoughts, images, or urges that cause significant anxiety or distress. The person does not want these thoughts — they are ego-dystonic, meaning they feel alien and at odds with the person's values.

Compulsions are repetitive behaviours or mental acts carried out in response to obsessions, aimed at reducing distress or preventing a feared outcome. They provide temporary relief, but over time make the OCD worse by reinforcing the obsession cycle.

Common presentations of OCD

  • Contamination OCD — fear of germs, dirt, or contamination; compulsive washing, cleaning, or avoidance
  • Harm OCD — intrusive thoughts about harming oneself or others (despite having no desire to do so); checking compulsions
  • Symmetry & ordering OCD — need for things to be arranged "just right"; straightening, ordering rituals
  • Checking OCD — repeated checking of locks, appliances, or actions out of fear something bad will happen
  • Pure O — OCD with predominantly mental compulsions (ruminating, mental reviewing, seeking reassurance internally)
  • Scrupulosity — obsessions about religious or moral wrongdoing; excessive prayer or confession rituals
  • Relationship OCD — constant doubt about one's relationship, partner's feelings, or one's own feelings

What OCD is not

OCD is not simply being particular, neat, or detail-oriented. The clinical condition involves significant distress, time consumption (typically more than 1 hour per day), and functional impairment. If it doesn't cause distress, it's not OCD.

Treatment

Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) is the gold-standard psychological treatment for OCD. It involves gradually exposing the patient to feared thoughts or situations while resisting the urge to perform compulsions. This breaks the obsession-compulsion cycle at its core.

Medication — SSRIs at higher doses than used for depression are effective for OCD. In treatment-resistant cases, other medication strategies are available. Medication and ERP together produce the best outcomes.

The key is that OCD requires a clinician who understands it specifically. Generic reassurance — which many people seek — is itself a compulsion and worsens OCD.

Note on intrusive thoughts

Having intrusive thoughts — including thoughts about harm, contamination, or morally distressing content — does not make a person dangerous or bad. These thoughts are the disorder, not a reflection of who the person is. This is one of the most important things for OCD patients to understand.
