What is depression?

Depression (Major Depressive Disorder) is a medical condition characterised by persistent low mood and a loss of interest or pleasure in activities, accompanied by a range of physical, cognitive, and emotional symptoms. It is caused by changes in brain chemistry, genetics, life events, and various biological factors — not personal weakness.

Symptoms

To meet the clinical criteria for depression, symptoms must be present for at least two weeks and represent a change from previous functioning:

  • Persistent low mood, sadness, or emptiness most of the day
  • Loss of interest or pleasure in previously enjoyed activities (anhedonia)
  • Significant changes in appetite or weight
  • Sleep disturbance — insomnia or sleeping too much
  • Psychomotor changes — slowing down or agitation
  • Fatigue and loss of energy
  • Feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt
  • Difficulty concentrating, thinking, or making decisions
  • Recurrent thoughts of death or suicide

Not all of these need to be present. Depression looks different in different people — in men, irritability and anger are often more prominent than sadness.

Types of depression

  • Major Depressive Disorder — the most common form; discrete episodes of depression
  • Persistent Depressive Disorder (Dysthymia) — lower-grade but chronic depression lasting 2+ years
  • Postpartum Depression — depression following childbirth, more severe than "baby blues"
  • Seasonal Depression — depressive episodes linked to seasonal changes
  • Depression with Psychotic Features — severe depression with hallucinations or delusions

Treatment at the clinic

Treatment is tailored to the type and severity of depression:

  • Mild depression: Psychotherapy (CBT), lifestyle interventions, and close monitoring
  • Moderate depression: CBT combined with antidepressant medication
  • Severe depression: Medication is typically the first-line intervention, with therapy added as the patient stabilises

The most commonly used medications are SSRIs (Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors) — they are safe, well-tolerated, and effective. Treatment duration is typically 6–12 months for a first episode.
