What is bipolar disorder?

Bipolar disorder is a mood disorder characterised by episodes of mania or hypomania (periods of abnormally elevated mood, energy, and activity) alternating with episodes of depression. It is not simply being "moody" — the episodes are distinct, often severe, and can last days to months.

Types

  • Bipolar I — involves at least one full manic episode, which may or may not be followed by depressive episodes. Manic episodes can be severe enough to require hospitalisation.
  • Bipolar II — involves hypomanic episodes (less severe than full mania) and major depressive episodes. Often misdiagnosed as depression because the hypomanic periods may not be recognised as abnormal.
  • Cyclothymia — a milder form with numerous periods of hypomanic and depressive symptoms that don't fully meet criteria for either episode type.

Symptoms of mania / hypomania

  • Abnormally elevated, expansive, or irritable mood
  • Markedly increased energy and activity
  • Decreased need for sleep (feeling rested after 2–3 hours)
  • Racing thoughts and rapid speech
  • Grandiosity or inflated self-esteem
  • Increased goal-directed activity or risk-taking behaviour
  • Poor judgment — financial decisions, sexual behaviour, impulsive actions

Why bipolar is frequently misdiagnosed

Most people with bipolar disorder seek help during depressive episodes, not manic ones. Because mania can feel good (initially), patients often don't report it — or don't recognise it as a problem. This is why many people with Bipolar II are treated for unipolar depression for years before the correct diagnosis is made. Antidepressants given without mood stabilisers can trigger manic episodes in bipolar patients — which is why accurate diagnosis is critical.

Treatment

Bipolar disorder is a lifelong condition that requires ongoing management, but the large majority of patients achieve good functional stability with appropriate treatment.

Mood stabilisers (lithium, valproate, lamotrigine) are the cornerstone of treatment. They reduce the frequency and severity of both manic and depressive episodes.

Atypical antipsychotics may be used for acute mania or as maintenance treatment.

Psychoeducation and psychotherapy — particularly CBT and family-focused therapy — significantly improve outcomes by helping patients recognise early warning signs and manage triggers.

Important

If you or a family member has been diagnosed with recurrent depression, especially if there are periods of unusually high energy, reduced sleep, or impulsive behaviour between depressive episodes — please discuss the possibility of bipolar disorder with a psychiatrist. The distinction matters enormously for treatment.
