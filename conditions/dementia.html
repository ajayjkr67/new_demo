What is dementia?

Dementia is not a single disease — it is an umbrella term for a group of conditions characterised by a decline in memory, language, problem-solving, and other cognitive functions severe enough to interfere with daily life. It is not a normal part of ageing, though the risk increases with age.

Types of dementia

  • Alzheimer's disease — the most common type, accounting for 60–70% of dementia cases. Characterised by progressive memory loss, language difficulties, and behavioural changes.
  • Vascular dementia — caused by reduced blood flow to the brain, often following strokes. Symptoms depend on which areas of the brain are affected and may have a more stepwise progression.
  • Lewy body dementia — involves protein deposits in the brain; characterised by fluctuating cognition, visual hallucinations, and Parkinsonian features.
  • Frontotemporal dementia — affects the frontal and temporal lobes; often presents with personality changes, behavioural disinhibition, and language difficulties rather than memory loss initially.

Early warning signs

Early signs of dementia that warrant evaluation:

  • Memory loss that disrupts daily life — forgetting recently learned information, asking the same question repeatedly
  • Difficulty with familiar tasks — managing finances, cooking a meal, using a phone
  • Confusion about time or place
  • Problems with language — difficulty finding words, following conversations
  • Poor judgment — unusual financial decisions, neglecting personal hygiene
  • Personality or mood changes — increased anxiety, suspicion, depression, or withdrawal

Normal ageing involves occasional forgetfulness (forgetting where you placed your keys). Dementia involves forgetting that you own keys.

Assessment and diagnosis

A thorough evaluation includes a detailed history from both the patient and a close family member, cognitive testing, physical and neurological examination, blood tests to rule out reversible causes, and brain imaging where appropriate.

Management

While most dementias are not currently curable, much can be done:

  • Medications can slow cognitive decline and manage behavioural symptoms in Alzheimer's disease
  • Managing cardiovascular risk factors reduces progression in vascular dementia
  • Behavioural interventions and structured daily routines improve quality of life
  • Caregiver education and support is essential — caring for someone with dementia is demanding, and carers need support too
  • Safety planning — driving, medication management, financial decisions
For families

If you have noticed concerning changes in an elderly family member's memory or behaviour, please seek evaluation early. Early diagnosis allows time for care planning, legal and financial arrangements, and accessing support services while the person can still participate in those decisions.
