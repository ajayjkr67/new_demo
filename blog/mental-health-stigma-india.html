Mental Health Stigma in India: Why We Stay Silent and How to Break It

6 min read Dr. Astik Mane

India has one of the largest mental health treatment gaps in the world. Studies estimate that over 80% of people with mental health conditions in India receive no treatment at all. The shortage of psychiatrists is part of the problem. But stigma is the bigger part.

Stigma operates on multiple levels — individual, family, and social — and each one reinforces the others. Understanding how it works is the first step to dismantling it.

What stigma looks like in Indian families

In many Indian households, mental illness is still associated with weakness, bad karma, family shame, or spiritual failing. A family member who develops depression or psychosis is often told to pray more, think positively, or simply not discuss it outside the home.

This silence has consequences. The affected person doesn't get treatment. The family doesn't get education about what's happening or how to help. And everyone involved absorbs the implicit message that mental illness is something to be hidden, not treated.

Marriage prospects are a particular pressure point. Many families avoid psychiatric diagnoses — even when treatment would genuinely help — because of the fear that it will affect matrimonial prospects for the affected person or their siblings. This fear is real, socially conditioned, and actively harmful.

The language we use

Language both reflects and reinforces stigma. In many Indian languages, the common words for "mad" or "mentally ill" are insults. Psychiatric hospitals are still colloquially called by terms that translate roughly to "madhouse." This linguistic landscape tells people what society thinks of mental illness — and it's not encouraging.

Contrast this with how we talk about physical health. Nobody is embarrassed to say they have diabetes or hypertension. These are just medical conditions that need management. We need to reach the point where saying "I have depression and I'm seeing a psychiatrist" carries the same neutrality.

Why men struggle particularly

In Indian culture, as in many others, emotional vulnerability is particularly discouraged in men. "Man up", "don't be weak", "handle it yourself" — these messages are absorbed from childhood. Men with depression frequently present with irritability, substance use, and physical complaints rather than sadness, partly because they've learned not to express emotional distress directly.

Men are significantly less likely to seek psychiatric help than women, and significantly more likely to die by suicide. These two facts are connected.

What's changing

There are genuine signs of progress. Mental health awareness among urban, educated young Indians has increased substantially in the past decade. Social media, whatever its other problems, has provided platforms for people to share their mental health journeys and reduce shame. Films and public figures have started speaking openly about their experiences.

But awareness campaigns don't automatically translate into treatment. The gap between knowing that mental health matters and actually walking into a psychiatrist's office remains wide for many people.

What you can do

If you are struggling: seek help without waiting for anyone's permission or approval. Your mental health is your business, not your family's reputation. A psychiatrist's consultation is confidential. Treatment works. The shame belongs to the stigma, not to you.

If someone you know is struggling: listen without judgment. Don't offer easy reassurances. Don't tell them to just be positive. Ask how you can help. Offer to go with them to an appointment if they're afraid to go alone. The most powerful thing you can do is make it clear that you will not think less of them for getting help.

Confidentiality

Everything discussed in a psychiatric consultation is strictly confidential. I do not share information with family members without the patient's consent, with employers, or with anyone else. If confidentiality is a concern for you, please raise it at the start of the consultation — it's a legitimate and common concern.

The treatment gap in India will close only when enough people decide that getting help is the rational, courageous thing to do — not the shameful thing. Every person who seeks treatment and talks openly about it makes it slightly easier for the next person. That's how stigma actually changes.
