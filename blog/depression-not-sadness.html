Depression Is Not Just Sadness — Understanding the Real Condition

5 min read Dr. Astik Mane

When someone says they're depressed, the most common response — even from well-meaning people — is some version of "just try to think positive" or "you have so much to be grateful for." This response reveals a fundamental misunderstanding of what depression actually is.

Depression is not an emotion. It is a medical condition. The distinction matters enormously — both for how sufferers understand themselves and for how people around them respond.

What depression actually is

Clinical depression — formally called Major Depressive Disorder — is a condition characterised by persistent changes in mood, thinking, physical functioning, and behaviour. It involves measurable changes in brain chemistry, particularly in systems involving serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine.

A person with depression cannot simply "cheer up" any more than a person with a broken leg can simply "walk it off." The brain's ability to experience pleasure, motivation, and positive emotion is genuinely impaired — not as a choice, and not because the person isn't trying hard enough.

What depression looks like — beyond sadness

Many people with depression don't describe their primary experience as sadness. They describe:

  • Emptiness — a flat, grey quality to experience rather than active sadness
  • Anhedonia — loss of pleasure in things that previously brought joy. Food, hobbies, relationships — nothing feels engaging.
  • Fatigue — a physical heaviness and exhaustion that rest doesn't fix
  • Cognitive slowing — difficulty concentrating, making decisions, or retaining information
  • Irritability — particularly common in men, who may present with anger rather than sadness
  • Physical symptoms — headaches, digestive problems, body aches with no medical cause
  • Disrupted sleep — either insomnia or sleeping far more than usual

Why the misunderstanding is harmful

When depression is treated as just a bad mood, several harmful things happen. The person suffering feels judged and misunderstood, which deepens the condition. They delay seeking treatment because they don't believe their experience is "serious enough." Family members respond with frustration rather than support. And the person often internalises the narrative that they are weak or lazy — which is false and damaging.

Depression significantly increases the risk of suicide. It impairs immune function, cardiovascular health, and overall physical health outcomes. It is not trivial, and it is not a character flaw.

Depression is highly treatable

This is the important counterpoint to everything above: depression responds well to treatment. Antidepressants are effective for moderate-to-severe depression. Cognitive Behavioural Therapy has strong evidence for mild-to-moderate cases and as a complement to medication in more severe cases. Most patients see meaningful improvement within 4–8 weeks of starting appropriate treatment.

The tragedy of depression is not that it's untreatable — it's that so many people never get treatment because of the stigma around it or the misunderstanding of what it is.

If you recognise this

If you have been experiencing these symptoms for more than two weeks — particularly if they are affecting your work, relationships, or daily functioning — please see a psychiatrist. Not a general physician, not a relative, not the internet. A specialist who can assess you properly and put together a treatment plan that works.

Depression does not have to be permanent. Most people who receive treatment recover and go on to live full, functional lives. The first step is getting the right help.
