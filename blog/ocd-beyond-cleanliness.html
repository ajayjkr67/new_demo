OCD Is Not Just About Cleanliness — The Full Picture

5 min read Dr. Astik Mane

"I like my desk perfectly organised — I'm so OCD about it." You've heard this. Maybe you've said it. The casual use of OCD as a synonym for being neat or particular has become so common that many people with real OCD go undiagnosed for years — either because they don't recognise what they have, or because they do but assume it couldn't be serious enough to warrant treatment.

Real OCD is not about preferences. It is not a personality quirk. It is a debilitating condition that can consume hours of a person's day and cause enormous suffering.

What OCD actually involves

OCD — Obsessive Compulsive Disorder — consists of two core components that occur together:

Obsessions are unwanted, intrusive thoughts, images, or urges that cause significant distress. The person does not want these thoughts. They don't reflect their values or desires. They simply intrude, repeatedly, against the person's will.

Compulsions are repetitive behaviours or mental acts performed to reduce the anxiety caused by obsessions. The temporary relief they provide reinforces the cycle, making the OCD stronger over time.

OCD is far wider than cleanliness

Contamination OCD — the kind that involves excessive handwashing and fear of germs — is real and common, but it's only one presentation. OCD can look like:

  • Harm OCD — intrusive thoughts about accidentally or intentionally harming yourself or others, despite having no desire to do so
  • Relationship OCD — constant doubt about whether you love your partner, whether they love you, whether you're in the right relationship
  • Pure O — OCD with primarily mental compulsions (ruminating, mental reviewing, seeking reassurance) rather than visible physical rituals
  • Scrupulosity — obsessions about religious or moral issues, fear of having sinned or been immoral
  • Symmetry and order OCD — this is the one most people mean when they joke about OCD, but in clinical form it causes genuine distress and can take hours per day
  • Health anxiety OCD — repeated checking for symptoms of illness, seeking repeated reassurance from doctors, inability to accept test results as reassuring

Why OCD is often missed

Many people with OCD — particularly those with Harm OCD or Pure O — are deeply ashamed of their thoughts and never tell anyone. They've concluded that having these thoughts makes them a bad person. They haven't considered that intrusive, unwanted thoughts are precisely what defines OCD — not any desire to act on them.

Clinicians who are not specialised in OCD sometimes miss it too, particularly when it presents without obvious rituals.

OCD is very treatable

Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP), a form of CBT specifically designed for OCD, has the strongest evidence base of any psychological treatment for the condition. Medication — specifically SSRIs at appropriate doses — is also effective. Most patients with OCD who receive proper treatment experience significant reduction in symptoms.

The key word is "proper." OCD requires a psychiatrist or therapist who understands it specifically. General reassurance-giving, which is what many well-meaning people and some poorly-trained therapists offer, actually worsens OCD by feeding the compulsion cycle.

If this sounds familiar

If you spend more than an hour a day on intrusive thoughts or rituals, if these thoughts or behaviours are distressing and interfere with your life, and especially if you have thoughts you're ashamed of — please see a psychiatrist. What you're experiencing is OCD, not evidence of who you are.
