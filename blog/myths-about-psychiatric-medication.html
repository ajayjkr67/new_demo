5 Myths About Psychiatric Medication — Debunked

6 min read Dr. Astik Mane

Fear of medication is one of the most common reasons people avoid or delay psychiatric treatment. Some of these fears come from genuine past experiences, some from things people have heard, and some from how psychiatry is portrayed in films and media. Most of them, however, are based on misunderstandings.

Here are five of the most common myths — addressed honestly.

Myth 1: "Psychiatric medication will change my personality"

This is the most common fear, and it's understandable. The idea that a pill could alter who you fundamentally are is unsettling.

The reality: psychiatric medications work on specific neurotransmitter systems that are dysregulated in mental health conditions. When they work correctly, they don't change your personality — they remove the symptoms that are obscuring it. Many patients say they feel "more like themselves" on medication, not less.

If a medication genuinely feels like it's flattening your personality or making you feel unlike yourself, that's important feedback to give your psychiatrist. Dosage adjustments or medication changes are always possible.

Myth 2: "I'll become dependent on them forever"

Some psychiatric medications do require gradual tapering rather than abrupt stopping — but this is true of many common medications like blood pressure drugs and steroids. It is not the same as addiction.

The duration of treatment varies significantly by condition. For a first episode of depression, medication is typically recommended for 6–12 months after remission. For recurrent or severe conditions, longer-term treatment may be recommended — but this is a clinical decision made based on your individual history, not a default outcome.

Many people take medication for a defined period, stabilise, and discontinue successfully under medical supervision.

Myth 3: "They'll make me numb and emotionless"

This fear often comes from older antipsychotic medications, which did cause significant sedation and blunting. Modern psychiatric medications are considerably more targeted, and emotional blunting — when it occurs — is a side effect that can usually be addressed by adjusting the dose or switching medications.

The goal of treatment is not to eliminate emotion. It is to regulate the extremes — to bring severe depression, uncontrollable anxiety, or psychotic symptoms under control so that you can function and feel again.

Myth 4: "Natural remedies are safer"

This framing assumes that "natural" equals safe and "pharmaceutical" equals harmful. Neither is automatically true. Many natural substances are toxic; many pharmaceuticals are extraordinarily safe.

Psychiatric medications prescribed in India go through regulatory approval processes. Their side effect profiles are documented and monitored. "Natural" alternatives — which can include anything from herbal supplements to lifestyle changes — may have a role in mild cases, but for clinical conditions like moderate-to-severe depression, bipolar disorder, or psychosis, there is no evidence-based natural substitute for medication.

This isn't a dismissal of lifestyle factors — sleep, exercise, and diet genuinely matter for mental health. But they are not a replacement for treatment when treatment is clinically indicated.

Myth 5: "Starting medication means I failed to handle it myself"

This is perhaps the most damaging myth, because it's rooted in stigma rather than logic.

Nobody says a diabetic patient "failed to handle it themselves" by taking insulin. Nobody tells a person with hypertension to just relax harder instead of taking antihypertensives. Mental health conditions have biological components. The brain is an organ. When it needs help, getting that help is not weakness — it's appropriate medical care.

A note on medication decisions

Not every patient who sees me needs medication. Therapy alone is appropriate for many conditions and many patients. When medication is recommended, it's because the clinical picture indicates that it will produce faster, more reliable relief than therapy alone. The decision is always made together with the patient, with a full explanation of the reasoning.

If you have specific concerns about medication, bring them to the consultation. They're worth discussing — not dismissing.
